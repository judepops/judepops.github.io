My research has centred on multi-omics: using machine learning to combine measurements that do not naturally fit together.

It started with genomics and splicing, in a summer project that came out of an article I wrote on alternative splicing and neurogenesis. My undergraduate thesis added transcriptomics and proteomics, and my postgraduate year added spatial transcriptomics and then metabolomics, which is the hardest layer to integrate.

That metabolomics work became a Python package using multivariate machine learning to model pathway features together. At Nucleome Therapeutics I moved to deep learning, fine-tuning genomic foundation models on chromatin accessibility, with the data engineering on AWS S3 and SageMaker.

My DPhil works a layer further up, in the epigenome, and uses all of it: machine learning, deep learning and multi-omics applied to gene regulation. The industry work was applied; this is the research side of the same question. Scaled across roughly 20,000 genes in the CD4+ T cell genome, an assay that resolves regulatory contacts to the base pair would give these models much better training data.
